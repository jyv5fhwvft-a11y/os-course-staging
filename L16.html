The Core Objective

Calculate total disk read access times factoring in seek time, rotational latency, and transfer rates, and compare architectural wear-leveling traits between mechanical platters and flash memory grids.

The Problem Hook: One storage device must physically swing a metal arm across a spinning magnetic plate before it can read a single byte. The other reads an electrical charge from a fixed silicon cell in microseconds. Both present the exact same "flat array of numbered sectors" to the operating system. How does the OS hide this violent architectural difference — and how do the physics of each shape every file system we build on top?

🎒 EVERYDAY SIMPLIFIED: THE VINYL RECORD PLAYER

The HDD Metaphor

🎵📀🪡
The Narrative

To play a specific song on a vinyl record, you must physically lift the tonearm, move it to the correct track position (seek time), and then wait for the record to spin around until the opening note passes underneath the needle (rotational latency). Only then does sound emerge (transfer). Moving metal takes real, slow, measurable time.

Execution Path

Every disk read has three sequential physical costs: arm movement, platter rotation, and byte streaming. There is no way to skip any of them.

Micro-Accessibility Context

This maps directly to the HDD cylinder/track/sector physical geometry and its three-part access-time formula.

🎒 EVERYDAY SIMPLIFIED: THE GRID OF LIGHT SWITCHES

The SSD Metaphor

💡💡💡
The Narrative

A Solid-State Drive is a wall of millions of tiny light switches. To read one, you don't move anything — you glance at its electrical state instantly. No arm, no platter, no waiting. But the switches have a rule: you can flip a switch on or off quickly, but to replace it you have to replace an entire row of ten thousand switches at once (block erasure). And every flip wears the switch out slightly.

Execution Path

Reads are effectively instant. Writes have asymmetry — page-level write, block-level erase. Erase cycles are finite.

Micro-Accessibility Context

This is the origin of the Flash Translation Layer (FTL) and wear leveling.

🎒 EVERYDAY SIMPLIFIED: THE SCATTERED LIBRARY

The Fragmentation Metaphor

📚🏃📖
The Narrative

Imagine a book whose chapters are separated and shelved across ten different library floors. To read it start to finish, you must run between floors ten times. Each trip costs the same slow walk. A defragmented book sits on one shelf — one walk, one read.

Execution Path

For HDDs, sequential access is dramatically faster than random access. For SSDs, the penalty for scattered layout is far smaller — but block erasure still favors locality.

Micro-Accessibility Context

This is exactly why file systems allocate contiguous blocks when possible, and why defragmentation matters on HDDs but is nearly irrelevant on SSDs.

🎒 EVERYDAY SIMPLIFIED: THE FINITE-USE NOTEBOOK

The Endurance Metaphor

📓✏️🔄
The Narrative

Imagine each page of a notebook can only be erased 1,000 times before the paper wears through. If you always write in the same spot, that page dies in a month. But if a manager rotates where notes go — spreading the wear across all pages — the notebook lasts for years.

Execution Path

SSD flash cells have bounded program/erase (P/E) cycles. Wear leveling continuously remaps logical blocks across physical cells to equalize the wear.

Micro-Accessibility Context

This is the reason SSDs ship with internal controllers that hide the remapping from the OS.

🎒 EVERYDAY SIMPLIFIED: THE HDD-AND-SSD DUALITY

The Summary Metaphor

⏱️ Mechanical Corridor

HDDs: seek-dominated, cheap per byte, slow random access, high capacity. Patient workhorses limited by physics of metal.

⚡ Silicon Corridor

SSDs: fast random access, endurance-limited, expensive per byte. Fast athletes bound by flash-cell lifetime.

Modern systems blend both — hot data on SSD, cold data on HDD. The choice reshapes every file system decision: allocation strategy, journaling frequency, defragmentation policy, and caching aggressiveness.

💻 SYSTEMS COHESION: MECHANICAL PLATTER TOPOLOGY

HDD Physical Geometry

Platter · Tracks · Arm · Head

Platter

A rigid magnetic disk storing bits as magnetized regions.

Track

A concentric circle on a platter surface.

Cylinder

The same track position across all platters in a stack.

Sector

The smallest addressable unit — historically 512 bytes, now 4 KB.

Head

The electromagnetic sensor positioned over a track.

The Verdict: Physical geometry dictates the access cost formula — arm movement, spin, then stream. No file-system abstraction can cheat metal.
💻 SYSTEMS COHESION: SEEK + ROTATE + TRANSFER

The Three-Component Access Time Formula

The Access Formula

T_access = T_seek + T_rotation + T_transfer

Variable Breakdown:

· T_seek: Time for the arm to reach the correct track. Averaged as ~1/3 of full-track sweep, typically 4–10 ms.

· T_rotation: Time for the sector to spin under the head. Averaged as ½ rotation: 30000 / RPM ms.

· T_transfer: Bytes read divided by bus rate. Typically sub-1 ms for small sectors.

For Random Small Reads

Seek + rotation dominate >99% of the total access cost.

Transfer is effectively negligible.

For Sequential Large Reads

Seek + rotation pay once at the start, then amortize over the entire run.

Transfer dominates — full disk bandwidth is achievable.

💻 SYSTEMS COHESION: FLOATING-GATE CELL TOPOLOGY

SSD NAND Flash Architecture

Each flash cell is a floating-gate transistor holding trapped charge. The charge level maps to bits:

SLC
1 bit
MLC
2 bit
TLC
3 bit
QLC
4 bit

SLC (1 bit)

2 voltage levels. Fast, long-lived (~100k cycles), expensive.

MLC (2 bits)

4 levels. Moderate performance, ~10k cycles.

TLC (3 bits)

8 levels. Slower, ~3k cycles. Ubiquitous in consumer SSDs.

QLC (4 bits)

16 levels. Slowest, ~1k cycles, high-density archival.

The Verdict: Density trades against endurance and speed. Every SSD is a balance chosen by its manufacturer — a decision you cannot override from the OS.
💻 SYSTEMS COHESION: THE ASYMMETRIC WRITE PENALTY

Page-Write, Block-Erase Asymmetry

The Asymmetric Rules

Read unit: Page (typically 4 KB)

Write unit: Page (but only to empty pages)

Erase unit: Block (typically 128–512 pages, ~512 KB–2 MB)

The Overwrite Problem

To modify a page that already holds data, the FTL must:

1. Copy the entire block into RAM

2. Modify the target page

3. Erase the block entirely

4. Write the block back, page by page

Write 4 KB
→
Erase 512 KB
+ Write 512 KB
Write amplification: 1 page update can require 128× or more physical work. This asymmetry is why FTL sophistication matters enormously.
💻 SYSTEMS COHESION: FTL AND WEAR-LEVELING ALGORITHMS

The Flash Translation Layer

FTL Responsibilities

Logical-to-physical mapping: Translates OS sector numbers into physical cell coordinates.

Garbage collection: Reclaims partially-used blocks by relocating live pages and erasing the rest.

Wear leveling: Rotates write destinations to equalize per-cell P/E counts.

Bad-block management: Detects and retires worn cells.

The Hidden Controller

The OS sees a flat array of numbered sectors.

The FTL is a shadow controller running an entire filesystem-of-its-own inside the drive.

Modern NVMe SSDs have multi-core ARM CPUs running sophisticated FTL firmware — some with full operating systems.

The OS has no visibility into this.

🔗 SYSTEM PIPELINE: RELIES ON MEMORY HIERARCHY

Core System Cohesion — Relies On L13 Swap Space

The Memory Hierarchy

Disk is the bottom of the pyramid built in Module 3. Swap space (L13 demand paging) lives here. Every page fault on the swap triggers the exact physical costs this lecture quantifies.

The Ratio

DRAM access: ~100ns. HDD seek: ~10,000,000ns. A ratio of ~100,000×.

That gap is why demand paging works (rare faults amortized) — and why thrashing (too many faults) collapses systems to a crawl.

The Architectural Bond

Every file-system policy in L17–L19 is a direct response to L16 physics. L16 is not just the substrate — it is the constraint that shapes every layer above.

Feeds Into L17–L19

L17 directory trees minimize seek hops. L18 inode block pointers favor contiguous allocation. L19 journaling must serialize intent writes to survive power loss — its cost is dominated by L16's physical access formula.

🔗 SYSTEM PIPELINE: FEEDS INTO FILE SYSTEMS AND JOURNALING

Core System Cohesion — Feeds Into Module 5

L17 · Directory Trees

Deep paths mean more lookups mean more possible seeks. This is why classic Unix designs shallow trees and caches directory blocks aggressively.

L18 · Inode Layouts

Contiguous block allocation (via direct pointers, then extents) minimizes seeks. This is why the 12-direct+indirect design (L18) is a direct response to L16's seek cost.

L19 · Journaling

Every journal write is a physical disk write. Journal design is dominated by the trade-off: flush frequently (safe, slow) vs. buffer (fast, risky).

The Dependency Chain

L16 physics → L17 directory structure → L18 inode pointers → L19 journal transactions. Every layer above is shaped by L16's access formula.

🔍 COMPREHENSIVE CRITIQUE: DISK STRATEGIES

Storage Management Across the Five Paradigms

Monolithic Storage Management

The block layer lives entirely in Ring 0, with an elevator I/O scheduler (CFQ, Deadline, or BFQ) that reorders requests to minimize seeks. The kernel has full authority over disk geometry.

Microkernel Storage Management

The block device driver runs as a user-space server (e.g., FUSE, or seL4's block servers). The kernel forwards I/O requests via IPC; scheduling policy is per-server. Slower, but crash-isolated.

Layered Storage Management

Strict VFS → block layer → device driver hierarchy. Each layer may only request services from the layer below; caching and journaling live in dedicated tiers.

Modular Storage Management

Core block layer in Ring 0, but pluggable I/O schedulers load as kernel modules (elevator=noop|deadline|bfq). Filesystem drivers are hot-swappable.

Hybrid Storage Management

Kernel handles block I/O and FTL translation for HDDs; for NVMe SSDs, the kernel sets up queue pairs that let userspace (via io_uring or SPDK) submit I/O directly to the drive's controller, bypassing the block layer entirely.

🏷️ SYSTEM LEXICON: MASTERING THE STORAGE VOCABULARY

Base Storage Terms

Term 01 · Seek Time

The physical time required for an HDD's mechanical read/write head arm to sweep and lock onto a target track cylinder; typically 4–10 ms for modern drives. Averaged as roughly 1/3 of the full-track sweep distance. Dominates random-access cost.

(The walk from the record player's needle resting spot to the song you want.)

Term 02 · Rotational Latency

The average time a drive partition waits for a specific sector block to spin under the active magnetic head sensor, computed as half a full rotation: 30000 / RPM milliseconds. For 7200 RPM drives, this is ~4.17 ms. The second-largest component of random access time.

(Waiting for the spinning record to bring the song's first note under the needle.)

🏷️ SYSTEM LEXICON: MASTERING THE STORAGE VOCABULARY

Advanced Storage Terms

Term 03 · Wear Leveling

An internal SSD optimization algorithm that dynamically remaps logical write address blocks to distribute flash cell erasure stress evenly across the device matrix, extending total drive lifespan. Without wear leveling, hot spots would fail in weeks; with it, SSDs last years.

(The manager rotating which notebook page you write on so no page wears out early.)

Term 04 · Flash Translation Layer

The embedded controller software inside an SSD that translates virtual OS sector read commands into physical cell array electrical voltage updates, while also managing garbage collection, wear leveling, and bad-block retirement. The OS has no visibility into the FTL's internal state.

(The interpreter who speaks "sector number" to the OS and "cell coordinate" to the flash chip.)

Term 05 · Write Amplification

The ratio of physical bytes written to flash cells to logical bytes the OS requested. Elevated by the page-write/block-erase asymmetry, requiring full block rewrites for single-page updates. A 4 KB update to a 512 KB block is a 128× write amplification event.

(Writing 1 page costs 512 pages of work because the whole block must be erased and rewritten.)

💻 CODE DISSECTION: C DISK LATENCY SIMULATOR

Calculating HDD Access Time and SSD Comparison

// Calculating HDD access time and simulating SSD behavior in C
// Compile: gcc -o disk disk.c && ./disk
#include <stdio.h>

void calculate_hdd_access(double rpm, double seek_ms,
                          double transfer_rate_mbps, int bytes) {
    // Rotational latency = half a full rotation: 30000 / RPM
    double rotation_ms = 30000.0 / rpm / 2.0;

    // Transfer time = bytes / (rate * 1e6) * 1000 ms
    double transfer_ms = (bytes / (transfer_rate_mbps * 1000000.0)) * 1000.0;

    double total_ms = seek_ms + rotation_ms + transfer_ms;

    printf("HDD (%.0f RPM, %.1fms seek, %d bytes):\n",
           rpm, seek_ms, bytes);
    printf("  Seek: %.3fms | Rotation: %.3fms | Transfer: %.4fms\n",
           seek_ms, rotation_ms, transfer_ms);
    printf("  TOTAL: %.3fms\n\n", total_ms);
}

void simulate_ssd_access(int bytes) {
    // Typical NVMe SSD: ~0.02ms access + ~3000 MB/s transfer
    double access_ms = 0.02;
    double transfer_ms = (bytes / 3000000000.0) * 1000.0;
    double total_ms = access_ms + transfer_ms;

    printf("SSD (NVMe, %d bytes):\n", bytes);
    printf("  Access: %.3fms | Transfer: %.5fms\n",
           access_ms, transfer_ms);
    printf("  TOTAL: %.4fms\n\n", total_ms);
}

int main() {
    int sector_512 = 512;
    int block_4k = 4096;

    printf("=== Storage Access Time Comparison ===\n\n");
    calculate_hdd_access(7200, 4.0, 100.0, sector_512);
    calculate_hdd_access(7200, 4.0, 100.0, block_4k);
    simulate_ssd_access(sector_512);
    simulate_ssd_access(block_4k);

    return 0;
}

Use code with caution.

💻 CODE DISSECTION: TRACING PHYSICAL LATENCY TERMS

Walking the Latency Arithmetic

rotation_ms = 30000.0 / rpm / 2.0

At 7200 RPM, one full rotation is 30000/7200 = 4.166 ms. Half-rotation average is 2.083 ms. This is the "average" position — the sector is uniformly distributed around the platter.

transfer_ms = (bytes / (rate * 1e6)) * 1000

A 512-byte read at 100 MB/s finishes in 0.005 ms — twenty times cheaper than rotation. A 4 KB read in 0.041 ms — still negligible compared to seek+rotation.

total_ms = 4.0 + 2.083 + 0.005 ≈ 6.09 ms

For 512 bytes. For 4 KB: 4.0 + 2.083 + 0.041 ≈ 6.12 ms. Reading 8× more data costs only 0.5% more time.

SSD equivalents

512 bytes: ~0.020 ms. 4 KB: ~0.021 ms. Two to three orders of magnitude faster than HDD.

The Dominant Cost

For HDD: seek + rotation dominate. Physical motion, not data volume. This is why every file system batches small reads into large sequential reads.

Why This Matters

The SSD has inverted decades of file-system optimization assumptions. Random access is nearly free; only write amplification and endurance matter. Modern file systems (Btrfs, ZFS) were designed for this new reality.

💻 TRY IT LIVE: AUDITING REAL DISK HARDWARE

Live Telemetry Audit

$ lsblk -d -o NAME,ROTA,SIZE,MODEL       # ROTA=1 means HDD, 0 means SSD
$ sudo hdparm -I /dev/sda | grep -i rpm  # Reports physical RPM
$ sudo smartctl -a /dev/sda | grep -i wear  # SSD wear-level count
$ sudo hdparm -t --direct /dev/sda       # Buffered read throughput
$ sudo hdparm -T /dev/sda                # Cached read throughput
$ df -h                                   # Filesystem usage summary
$ cat /sys/block/sda/queue/rotational    # 0=SSD, 1=HDD
$ cat /sys/block/sda/queue/scheduler     # I/O scheduler currently active

lsblk -o NAME,ROTA is the fastest way to see whether a device is mechanical or flash. smartctl reports SSD health metrics that mechanical disks simply do not have.

🏆 RETROSPECTIVE: SYLLABUS DISSECTION SUMMARY

Grand Lecture Summary

01

HDD access time is dominated by physical motion — seek time and rotational latency cost 99% of a random read. For a 512-byte sector on a 7200 RPM drive, the total is ~6 ms, of which the actual transfer is only 0.005 ms. The mechanical platter is a fundamentally seek-bound device.

02

SSDs eliminate moving parts and land access latency in microseconds (~0.02 ms), but pay an asymmetric penalty: page-write, block-erase. The Flash Translation Layer and wear leveling hide this from the OS, at the cost of write amplification — a 4 KB update can cost 512 KB of physical work.

03

Every file-system design decision in L17–L19 — contiguous allocation, directory trees, inode block pointers, and journaling — is a direct response to the physical constraints introduced in this lecture. Storage physics is not a curiosity — it is the forcing function that shapes every layer above.
